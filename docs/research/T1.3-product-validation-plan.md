# T1.3 — Product validation plan

- **Status:** PROPOSED — preparation only; no research results
- **Prepared:** 2026-11-21
- **Related task:** T1.3
- **Dependency state:** D0.1 is complete
- **Approval state:** Sample, safeguards, thresholds, and research materials require founder approval before use

## 1. Purpose and evidentiary limits

This plan prepares lean research into whether Veyra solves a sufficiently important practical communication problem for an international audience aged 13+. It does not establish demand, usability, willingness to pay, legal eligibility, content accuracy, audio licensing, or product-market fit. No participant has been recruited or interviewed, and no result is recorded here.

Veyra remains an English-first commercial language-learning SaaS with eight target-language tracks across seven languages. The preferred initial rollout is a free beta and a later freemium model is proposed, not approved. The first research wave may prioritize three tracks for sequencing, but the other five remain on the roadmap.

## 2. Known facts and hypotheses

### Confirmed project facts

- The intended audience includes teenagers aged 13–17 and adults aged 18+ internationally; country-by-country eligibility is unresolved.
- The intended use contexts are relocation, work, travel, and everyday life.
- English is the initial instructional language; no translated lesson content exists.
- Eight target-language tracks remain in scope: Spanish, French, German, Mandarin Chinese, Korean, Brazilian Portuguese, European Portuguese, and Japanese.
- No verified customers, interviews, payments, reviewed lesson content, licensed audio, or demand evidence exists.

### Hypotheses to test

1. Learners with a specific near-term situation value situation-based communication more than broad, decontextualized study.
2. Relocators and established residents experience recurring high-consequence communication gaps that general-purpose tools do not fully resolve.
3. Workers and job seekers need respectful, role-specific phrases and comprehension practice, not only vocabulary recall.
4. Travelers value short, offline-tolerant preparation for predictable interactions.
5. Teenagers and adults can use the same core lesson structure, but require different trust, onboarding, autonomy, payer, and support treatment.
6. A short flow of context, explanation, reviewed audio, practice, feedback, saved progress, and review is understandable and worth repeating.
7. A free beta can earn a concrete follow-up commitment; a later freemium offer may have value to eligible adult purchasers or guardians, but hypothetical price answers alone will not validate payment demand.

## 3. Primary learner segments and testable needs

Segments are defined by an immediate job rather than nationality or demographic stereotypes. Age cohort, target track, country, accessibility needs, and interface/instruction preferences are research dimensions within each segment.

| Segment | Testable need | Evidence to seek | Failure signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Near-term relocators and accompanying family members | Prepare for housing, transport, registration, healthcare, school, shopping, and neighbor interactions before and after arrival. | A recent or imminent situation, current workaround, consequence, recurrence, and a concrete preparation behavior. | Problems are rare, low consequence, or already solved satisfactorily. |
| International workers and job seekers | Understand and respond in workplace, interview, scheduling, safety, customer, and colleague situations. | Specific communication breakdowns, role constraints, existing practice, and need for respectful regional phrasing. | General translation or employer support is consistently sufficient. |
| Short-stay travelers | Complete predictable time-sensitive interactions with limited study time and connectivity. | A recent trip example, preparation window, offline/mobile constraints, and repeated high-priority situations. | Learners prefer one-off translation and do not intend to prepare or review. |
| Everyday-life residents and family/community participants | Build confidence for recurring errands, services, social contact, appointments, and school/community interactions. | Repeated avoided or delegated interactions and willingness to practise the same situation over time. | No recurring gap or no repeat-use motivation exists. |

Teen participants must be analyzed separately from adults. Research must identify who initiates use, who controls the device/account, who provides consent where required, who pays, and when a guardian or trusted adult is involved. These are questions, not assumptions.

## 4. Proposed research method

### Gate before recruitment

Founders must approve the script, participant matrix, success/failure thresholds, recording policy, incentive, storage location, access, retention/deletion date, and anonymization method. Before any teenager is contacted, a qualified review must establish lawful and age-appropriate recruitment, consent/assent or guardian involvement where applicable, safeguarding/escalation, incentives, data minimization, and deletion requirements for the participant's country. If that approval is absent, teen recruitment does not begin and T1.3 cannot complete.

Do not collect exact birth dates, identity documents, school names, precise addresses, immigration status, health details, or unnecessary free-form personal information. Record only the minimum age band, broad country/region permitted by the approved plan, relevant context, target track interest, accessibility needs volunteered for the session, and research evidence.

### Proposed lean sample

- **Problem interviews:** 18 participants: 6 teenagers aged 13–17 and 12 adults aged 18+, subject to the teen-research gate.
- **Coverage:** at least three countries; at least four participants contributing relevant evidence to each of relocation, work, travel, and everyday-life contexts; at least four participants with accessibility, low-bandwidth, shared-device, or other access needs relevant to product design. One participant may contribute to more than one context, but counts are reported transparently.
- **Concept walk-throughs:** 8 of the interviewed participants, balanced as four teenagers and four adults where approved, using a non-functional, original lesson-flow prototype with no account creation and no unlicensed audio.
- **Sampling limit:** this purposive qualitative sample can expose problems and failure modes; it cannot estimate international market prevalence or statistical demand.

### Proposed decision thresholds

These thresholds are proposals requiring founder approval before the first session. Changing them after results are visible must be recorded with a reason and the original retained.

- At least 12 of 18 participants describe a relevant recent or imminent real-world situation, its consequence, and an action they took or attempted; at least two qualifying accounts must appear in each of the four contexts.
- At least 9 of 18 identify a concrete limitation in an alternative they actually used, rather than only expressing a preference for Veyra's description.
- At least 6 of 8 concept participants complete the proposed lesson sequence and accurately explain what was learned, what feedback meant, and what would be reviewed next, without moderator rescue.
- At least 9 of 18 make a verifiable low-risk follow-up commitment, such as agreeing to a safeguarded prototype session or opting into a permitted beta-contact list. Courtesy statements do not count.
- Pricing evidence is reported separately for eligible adult purchasers, guardians where approved, and teenagers. T1.3 requires a documented pricing decision, but no paid-demand claim may rely only on answers to hypothetical willingness-to-pay questions.
- A failure on any threshold is retained and leads to a documented narrow, revise, retest, or stop decision; evidence is not discarded to manufacture a pass.

## 5. Interview questions

Ask questions 1–7 before showing the concept. Keep prompts neutral and request examples rather than agreement.

1. Tell me about the last time you needed another language for relocation, work, travel, or everyday life.
2. What were you trying to understand or communicate, and what happened?
3. How did you prepare or solve it at the time?
4. Which apps, classes, people, translation tools, or other alternatives did you use, and how often?
5. What worked well, and where did the alternative fail or create extra effort?
6. Which similar situation is likely to happen next, and how important is it to handle it yourself?
7. What limits your practice today—for example time, cost, confidence, device, connection, accessibility, privacy, or support?
8. After this lesson-flow walk-through, what do you think each step does, and where would you hesitate or stop?
9. What specific result would make you return to a free beta, and what follow-up would you be willing to take now?
10. If a later version charged for some features, who would decide and pay, what would they compare it with, and what price or structure would make it clearly not worth considering?

For teenagers, question 10 must follow the approved script and must not solicit a purchase, payment details, or an unsupported assumption that the teenager can contract or pay.

|---|---|---|---|

## 7. Selecting the first three tracks

Do not choose tracks from founder preference, global population, competitor availability, or search volume alone. After the interviews, score every one of the eight tracks against the same evidence rubric. Use a 1–5 scale with an evidence citation for every score; an unsupported score is `UNKNOWN`, not zero.

| Criterion | Proposed weight | Required evidence |
|---|---:|---|
| Repeated urgent practical need in the approved initial contexts | 25% | Interview incidents, consequences, frequency, and time horizon. |
| Ability to recruit and retain the intended beta cohort | 15% | Permitted follow-up commitments and reachable participant pool, split by age. |
| Qualified linguistic review capacity | 15% | Identified reviewer candidates, locale coverage, availability, and estimated cost; not an invented approval. |
| Licensed, reviewed audio feasibility | 15% | Speaker/source options, rights path, production effort, and accessibility alternative. |
| Content/locale risk and regional-variant clarity | 10% | Script, register, romanization, regional variants, safety-sensitive situations, and specialist-review needs. |
| Learning-flow fit without speech recognition or paid AI | 10% | Concept-test evidence for deterministic exercises and review. |
| Delivery/access constraints | 5% | Font/script, mobile input, bandwidth, device, and accessibility evidence. |
| Strategic learning value across all four contexts | 5% | Coverage matrix showing reusable situations without pretending tracks are interchangeable. |

The proposed first wave is the three highest evidence-supported tracks only after founder review of the matrix, risk/cost constraints, and contradictory evidence. Ties remain unresolved until more evidence is collected. The other five tracks retain roadmap status and their scores; sequencing is not cancellation.

## 8. Testable lesson flow

1. **Situation:** state a specific real-world goal and context. Pass when the learner can say what outcome they are preparing for.
2. **Explanation:** give a concise English-first meaning, register/cultural note, and accessibility-equivalent text. Pass when the learner can explain when the phrase is appropriate.
3. **Reviewed audio:** play/pause/replay a human-reviewed, correctly licensed target-locale asset with transcript; never autoplay. Pass when provenance and review records exist and failure leaves the text lesson usable. Until then, audio is omitted and this step remains untested.
4. **Practice:** use deterministic selection, matching, ordering, or typed-response exercises against reviewed answers. Pass when expected answers and accepted variants are explainable without an AI service.
5. **Feedback:** explain why an answer is correct or incorrect and offer a retry without invented pronunciation scoring. Pass when every response has a reviewed, accessible feedback state.
6. **Saved progress:** record completion, attempts, and difficult items only after authorized implementation. In a prototype, label this as a simulation and do not claim persistence. Pass later through reload/cross-device and authorization tests.
7. **Review:** return saved/difficult items through a documented deterministic rule. Pass when due items, successful recalls, retries, and no-item states are predictable and understandable.

The end-to-end concept test asks the learner to identify the goal, complete the practice, interpret feedback, locate saved progress, and predict what will be reviewed next. Research results must be split by age cohort and relevant access needs.

## 9. Capabilities that do not require speech recognition, paid AI, or unlicensed audio

- Situation and track navigation, English-first explanations, reviewed text/transcripts, and cultural/register notes after the linguistic workflow approves them.
- Deterministic multiple-choice, matching, ordering, cloze, and typed-answer exercises with human-authored accepted variants.
- Answer feedback, retry, completion criteria, difficult-item marking, saved progress, and a simple documented review schedule.
- Text-first concept tests, interface-language selection prototypes, keyboard/reflow/reduced-motion tests, and accessibility review.
- Downloadable or low-bandwidth text experiences and honest audio-unavailable states.
- Licensed reviewed audio playback later, without recording the learner or scoring pronunciation.

Veyra cannot claim listening or pronunciation quality without reviewed licensed audio. It cannot claim speaking assessment without a validated assessment method. Text-only or silent prototypes may validate comprehension of the flow, but they do not validate the audio step.
